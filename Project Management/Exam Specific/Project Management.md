# 1 Introduction
## 1.1 Define Project
- A project is a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result. 

- in detail, project has
	- **Temporary endeavor**: a project is a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service or result
	- **defined start and end**: it has a clearly defined beginning and a finite end, unlike ongoing operational work
	- **Unique output**: every project produces something that is non-routine and distinct from existing products or services
	- **operates within constraints**: projects function under specific limitations, primarily time, cost, and scope
	- **non-repetitive nature:** projects are not repetitive operational tasks; each project is distinct and involves unique activities
	- **Resource Integration**: requires coordinated use of diverse resources including man, machine, material, money and time.
	- **Progressive Elaboration:** project details become progressively more detailed and refined as the project moves forward.
	- **Goal-Oriented**: every project has a specific objective or deliverable that defines its success-criteria

## 1.3 Define Project Management
- Project management is the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements.

- in detail, it is:
	- **Application of Knowledge**: Project management is the application of knowledge skill tools and techniques to project activitiies
	- **Meeting Requirements:** It focuses on meeting the specific requirements and objectives of a project
	- **Balancing Constraints**: involves balancing the competing constraints of scope, schedule, budget, quality, resources and risk
	- **Structured Framework:** Provides a systematic and structured framework for managing work from initiation to closure
	- **Process Groups:** Utilizes five process groups: Initiating, Planning, Executing, monitoring & Controlling, and closing
	- **Knowledge areas**: integrates ten knowledge areas including scope, time, cost, quality, resource, communication, risk, procurement, stakeholder and integration management
	- **stakeholder satisfaction**: Aims to satisfy the needs and expectations of all stakeholders including customer, sponsor and team members
	- **Proactive control**: emphasizes proactive identification and mitigation of risks, issues, and changes throughout the project lifecycle.
---
## 1.4 Importance of Project Management
- **Increases Success Rate:** provides a structured framework that significantly improves the likelihood of meeting objectives, schedule, and budget
- **manages triple constraints**: Offers tools and processes to balance, Time, Cost, Scope that enables informed trade-off decisions.
- **Optimized Resource Utilization**: Ensures resources (people, budget, materials) are allocated and used efficiently, minimizes waste
- **Manages Risk Proactively**: Identifies, assesses and mitigates potential problems before they derail the project.
- **Facilitates Communication**: Establishes clear communication channels and reporting structures, ensuring all stakeholders are aligned.
- **Ensures Quality**: Incorporates processes for quality planning and control, ensuring deliverables meet required specifications.
- **Enhances Customer Satisfaction:** Delivers intended product on time and within budget, building trust with the client.
- **Provides Competitive Advantage**: Organizations that manage projects well can execute strategies faster and adapt to market changes
- **Addresses IT-specific Challenges**: Helps navigate issues like staff turnover, information security, connectivity problems, and regulatory requirements.
- **Facilitates Change Management**: Provides a controlled process for handling requirement changes, which are common in IT projects.
---
## 1.5 Characteristics of Project
1. **Specific Objective**: Every project has a clear, defined goal or deliverable. Success is measured by achieving this objective
2. **Temporary nature:** a project has a finite lifespan with a definite beginning and end. it progresses through stages like formulation, planning, execution and closure
3. **Non-routine and Non-repetitive**: Projects are not repetitive operational tasks. Each project is distinct with unique activities and risks
4. **Constraints**: Projects operate within boundaries, most notably the Triple Constraints of Time, Cost and Scope/Quality.
5. **Uniqueness**: No two projects are identical. They involve unique sets of activities, risks, stakeholders, and environments.
6. **Flexibility:** Projects operate in dynamic environments, requiring adaptability to manage risks, changes and unforeseen challenges
7. **Resource integration**: Requires coordinated use of diverse resources: man, Machine, Material, Money, and Minutes (Time).
8. **Teamwork**: Brings together specialists from different disciplines who must collaborate effectively under a project manager.
9. **Planning & Control**: Success depends on robust planning and a system for monitoring progress and implementing corrective actions
10. **Contracting and subcontracting**: Complex projects rely on formal contracts (lump-sump, unit price, turnkey) with external vendors
11. **Beneficiaries**: Every project is intended for a specific group of end-users or beneficiaries impacted by its output.
12. **Risk and Uncertainty**: Highest at the start of the project, and progressively decreases as more information becomes known.

## 1.6 Triple Constraints
### 1.6.1 1..1 The constraints
1. **Time Constraint:** the schedule for the project, including deadlines for milestones and final delivery of the product
2. **Cost Constraint**: The approved budget, including all resources, labor, materials, overheads and contingency reserves
3. **Scope Constraint**: The defined work required to achieve the project's objective, including the required level of quality and features.
### 1.6.2 Principle & Examples
- **Core principle**: you cannot change one side of the triangle without impacting the other two sides.
- **example 1:** if scope is increased (adding a new feature), you will likely need more time, and/or a higher cost
- **example 2**: if time is reduced (accelerating the schedule), you may need to increase cost (add resources) or reduce scope
- **example 3**: if cost is reduced (cutting budget), you may need more time or reduce scope/quality
- **PM's Role**: the project manager's primary responsibility is to manage these trade-offs to achieve a successful outcome
- **Stakeholder Communication**: Triple constraints provide a framework for discussing changes and their impacts with stakeholders.

# 2 Chapter 2 PMBOK
## 2.1 What is PMBOK
- **Definition**: PMBOK is a collection of processes and knowledge areas generally accepted as best practice within project management.
- **Publisher**: It is published by the Project Management Institute (PMI), the leading professional organization for project management.
- **Universality**: The fundamentals of project management remain the same regardless of the industry, whether construction, software, engineering or automotive
- **Unique Concepts**: Many concepts within PMBOK are unique to project management such as CPM (Critical Path Method), PERT, WBS (Work Breakdown Structure) and EVA (Earned Value Analysis)
- **Overlap with general management**: Some areas overlap with general management disciplines, including financial forecasting, organizational behavior and planning techniques
- **Framework structure**: the pmbok framework organizes project management into 5 process groups and 10 knowledge areas
- **process components**: each process is defined by three components: Inputs (documentations/plans needed), Tools & Techniques (mechanisms applied), and Outputs (documents/ deliverables produced).
- **Industry Standard**: It serves as the global standard for project management professionals seeking certification like PMP (Project Management Professional)
---
## 2.2 knowledge contents that fall under PMBOK
/ Describe what bodies of knowledge are required by PM to contribute for a successful project implementation
1. **Application Area knowledge**: understanding the specific domain, industry standards, and regulations relevant to the project (e.g., IT, construction, healthcare)
2. **Standards and regulations**: knowledge of applicable laws, compliance requirements and industry standards that govern project execution
3. **Understanding the project environment**: Awareness of internal, task and external environmental factors that influence project success
4. **General Management Knowledge**: Skills in planning, organizing, staffing, coordinating, leading, controlling, financial management, and HR management
5. **Interpersonal Skills**: Abilities including communication, influencing, negotiation, conflict resolution and relationship building
6. **Project Management Process Groups**: Knowledge of initiating, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling and closing processes
7. **Project Integration Management**: Coordinating and unifying all elements of the project as a central hub of control.
8. **Project Scope Management**: Ensuring the project includes all required work and only the required work for successful completion.
9. **Project Schedule Management**: Processes to manage timely completion of the project using techniques like CPM and PERT
10. **Project Cost Management**: Planning, estimating, budgeting, financing and controlling costs throughout the project lifecycle.
11. **Project Quality Management**: Incorporating quality policy to ensure the project meets its objectives and stakeholder expectations
12. **Project Risk & Procurement Management**: Identifying and managing risks, plus purchasing products/services from outside the project team.

## 2.3 Stakeholders
- **Definition**: A stakeholder is any individual, group, or party that has an interest in an organization and the outcomes of its actions or projects.
- **Common Examples**: Employees, customers, shareholders, suppliers, communities, governments, and regulatory bodies.
- **Project Specific Stakeholders**: For a project, stakeholders include the project sponsor, project manager, project team, client/customer, contractors, and end-users
- **Positive or Negative Interest**: Stakeholders can be positively impacted (benefiting from project success) or negatively impacted (affected by project outcomes)
- **Influence Over Project**: Stakeholders have varying levels of influence, from high (sponsor, client) to low (end-users, general public)
- **Expectation Management:** A key responsibility of the project manager is to identify stakeholders and manage their needs, wants, and expectations.
- **Communication Requirement**: Different stakeholders need different types of information at different times, requiring a tailored communication plan.
- **Stakeholder Register**: A formal document that lists all stakeholders, their roles, influence levels, expectations, and communication preferences.

## 2.4 Environments in Project
### 2.4.1 Internal Environment
- **Location**: within the project's direct control and immediate boundaries of the project team
- **Direct Management**: Can be directly managed and altered by the project team without any external approval
- **Project Objectives:** The specific goals and targets that the project aims to achieve within defined parameters
- **Internal Constraints:** Limitations such as budget boundaries, deadline requirements, and resource availability
- **Project Structure and Governance**: The organizational hierarchy, reporting lines, and decision-making framework within the project.
- **Allocated Resources**: The team members, equipment, budget and materials assigned specifically to the project.
### 2.4.2 Task Environment / Stakeholder Environment
- **Location**: Immediately surrounding the project, forming the immediate operational context
- **Direct Interaction**: Directly interacts with and affects project activities on a regular basis
- **Partial Control**: The project can influence these elements but cannot fully control them.
- **Key Stakeholders include**: Client/Customer, contractors and suppliers, consultants, competitors, and government/regulatory bodies
- **Dynamic Relationships**: Relationships with these stakeholders require active management and continuous communication.
### 2.4.3 External Environment
The PESTLE Framework
- **Political Factor**: Political Stability, government systems, institutional philosophies, and policies affecting project operations
- **Economic Factors**: Economic systems, inflation rates, interest rates, market conditions and regional trade group influences.
- **Socio-Cultural Factors**: Demographics, cultural norms, social institutions, pressure groups and lifestyle changes
- **Technological Factors**: Level of technological advancement, rate of technological change, R&D activity, and technological transfer possibilities.
- **Legal** and **Environmental Factors**: relevant laws, labor laws, courts, administrative bodies, plus climate change, geography and ecological constraints

## 2.5 How External environment influences ICT Project
1. **Political Instability:** Changes in government or policies can halt or redirect ICT projects, especially those involving public data or government contracts.
2. **Economic Downturns:** Inflation, interest rate changes, or market crashes can reduce project budgets, freeze hiring, or cancel non-essential IT initiatives.
3. **Technological Advancements:** Rapid emergence of new technologies (e.g., AI, cloud, blockchain) can make current project approaches obsolete mid-development.
4. **Legal Compliance Requirements:** New data protection laws (like GDPR) or cybersecurity regulations may force significant changes to project scope and design.
5. **Socio-Cultural Shifts:** Changes in user behavior, digital literacy levels, or privacy concerns affect how end-users adopt and interact with IT systems.
6. **Labor Market Conditions:** Shortages of skilled IT professionals (developers, data scientists) can delay projects and increase costs.
7. **Currency Fluctuations:** For projects with offshore resources or international software licenses, exchange rate changes impact budget and procurement.
8. **Environmental Disasters:** Natural events like earthquakes, floods, or pandemics disrupt infrastructure, power supply, and team availability.
9. **International Trade Policies:** Tariffs, sanctions, or trade restrictions affect procurement of hardware, software licenses, and cloud services.
10. **Competitive Pressure:** Actions by competitors launching similar products can force scope changes or accelerate timelines for ICT projects.

## 2.8 Key to failure is trying to please everybody.
### 2.8.1 Relevance of the statement
1. **Unrealistic Expectations:** Trying to please every stakeholder (users, management, developers, clients) in an IT project leads to scope creep and conflicting requirements.
2. **Impossible Trade-offs:** IT projects already balance triple constraints; adding "please everyone" makes trade-offs impossible and decisions paralyzing.
3. **Priority Setting:** Successful IT project managers must prioritize based on business value, not attempt to satisfy every minor request from every stakeholder.
4. **Clear Decision Making:** The statement emphasizes that strong, sometimes unpopular, decisions are necessary for project success rather than consensus-seeking delays.
5. **Managing Stakeholder Conflicts:** Different stakeholders want different things (security vs. usability, speed vs. features); pleasing all is functionally impossible.
6. **Project Success Definition:** Success is meeting agreed objectives, not making everyone happy, unhappy stakeholders may still receive a successful project.
### 2.8.2 Traits of Successful Manager
1. **Leads by Example:** Demonstrates desired behaviors, work ethic, and commitment, inspiring the team to follow suit.
2. **Visionary and Decisive:** Has clear project vision and makes timely decisions even with incomplete information.
3. **Technically Competent:** Possesses sufficient technical understanding of IT systems to earn team respect and make informed decisions.
4. **Good Communicator and Motivator:** Expresses ideas clearly, listens actively, and energizes the team to overcome barriers.
5. **Stands Up to Top Management:** Protects the team from unrealistic demands and advocates for necessary resources and time.
6. **Supports Team Members:** Encourages new ideas, provides growth opportunities, and backs team members in difficult situations.
7. **Result-Oriented:** Focuses on achieving final project objectives and delivering business value, not just completing task lists.
8. **Negotiation and Conflict Resolution:** Resolves disagreements and achieves consensus to find optimal solutions for project success.
9. **Problem Solver:** Looks beyond immediate team to understand broader organizational context and root causes.
10. **Global Literacies:** Ability to work effectively in cross-cultural environments, especially important for distributed IT teams and offshore resources.

## 2.13 Expert Judgement
1. **Definition:** Expert judgement is a tool and technique used in project management that relies on the knowledge and experience of individuals or groups with specialized expertise.
2. **Application Areas:** Used across all process groups for activities like developing project charters, creating scope statements, estimating costs and durations, and identifying risks.
3. **Sources of Expertise:** Can come from internal sources (other departments, senior management, subject matter experts within the organization) or external sources (consultants, industry experts, professional bodies).
4. **Historical Project Data:** Experts use lessons learned and historical information from previous similar projects to inform current project decisions.
5. **Industry Best Practices:** Incorporates knowledge of industry standards, methodologies, and proven approaches relevant to the specific project type.
6. **Disciplined vs. Intuitive:** Combines structured analytical thinking with intuitive experience-based judgement for balanced decision making.
7. **Validation of Estimates:** Expert judgement helps validate estimates and plans developed through other techniques like analogous or parametric estimating.
8. **Risk Assessment:** Experts identify potential risks that may not be apparent through standard checklists or documentation review.
9. **Stakeholder Involvement:** Key stakeholders often serve as experts, providing domain-specific knowledge about requirements and constraints.
10. **Documentation:** Expert judgement inputs and decisions should be documented to maintain transparency and provide rationale for project choices.

## 2.14 Decision Tree Analysis
| Feature                         | Description                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Definition                      | A decision tree, aka decision tree analysis, is a diagram that helps identify outcomes resulting from a collection of related choices, used for expected monetary value (EMV) study |
| Visual Structure                | The diagram uses a tree like model of decisions (branches) and their possible consequences, including chance event outcomes and resource costs                                      |
| Decision Nodes                  | Represented as squares (□), the points where the project manager must make a choice between alternative actions                                                                     |
| Chance Nodes                    | Represents as circles (○), these points where multiple possible outcomes may occur based on certain events with assigned probabilities.                                             |
| End Nodes                       | Represented as triangles (△), these terminal points showing the final outcome or payoff for a particular path                                                                       |
| EMV Calculation                 | Each branch is assigned a probability and monetary value; EVM is calculating by multiplying probability by impact and summing across all branches.                                  |
| Risk Quantification             | Helps quantify the potential financial impact of different risk response strategies before committing to a course of action.                                                        |
| Comparison of<br>Alternatives   | Allows project managers to compare the expected value of different side-by-side to select the optimal path                                                                          |
| Sensitivity<br>Analysis Support | Decision trees can be combined with sensitivity analysis to understand how changes in assumptions affect optimal decisions.                                                         |
| Complex Decision Simplification | Breaks down complex, multi-stage decisions into manageable, sequential choices that are easier to analyze                                                                           |
| Probability requirement         | Requires reliable estimates of event probabilities, which can be derived from historical data, expert judgement, or Delphi Technique                                                |
| Limitation                      | Becomes very complex with many branches, and may oversimplify real-world interdependencies between decisions                                                                        |

- **Certification Authority**: PMI certifies project management professionals (PMP, CAPM, etc.), validating their knowledge and experience
- **Research and Resources**: PMI conducts research on project management trends, publishes best practices, and provides educational resources.
- **Community and Networking**: PMI creates a global community of project management practitioners who share knowledge and advance the profession.
---
## 3.3 PMI Framework
- **Definition**: PMI Framework is a basic structure for understanding project management that organizes processes, knowledge areas, and lifecycle phases.
- **Stakeholders Need and Expectation**: The framework begins with identifying stakeholders (individuals or groups with interest in project outcomes) and their needs
- **Core Knowledge Areas**(4): Includes Scope Management, Time Management, Cost Management and Quality Management as fundamental functions
- **Facilitating Knowledge Areas** (4): Includes human resource management, communication management, risk management and procurement management as supporting functions
- **Integrative Knowledge Area** (1): Project integration management coordinates and unifies all core and facilitating functions
- **Tools and Techniques**: Methods applied across knowledge areas to increase understanding and execute processes effectively (e.g., software, meetings, data analysis)
- **Process Groups** (5): The framework organizing activities into initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and Closing
- **Input-Process-Output Model**: Each process is defined by inputs (documents needed), Tools & Techniques (actions applied) and Outputs (deliverables produced)
- **Core vs Facilitating Functions**: Core functions directly manage the work product; facilitating functions support the core functions.
- **Project Success Goal**: The framework aims to attain project success through integrated application of knowledge areas and process groups.
- **Portfolio Management Connection**: PMI framework extends to portfolio management treating IT assets as financial assets for balanced investment
- **Continuous Improvement:** The framework emphasizes learning from lessons learned and applying best practices across projects.
---
## 3.4 Knowledge Areas of PMI
1. **Project Integration Management:** The processes to coordinate and unify all elements of the project; acts as the central hub for decision making and change control.
2. **Project Scope Management:** Ensures the project includes all the work required (and only the required work) to complete it successfully.
3. **Project Schedule (Time) Management:** Processes to manage the timely completion of the project using techniques like CPM, PERT, and Gantt charts.
4. **Project Cost Management:** Processes involved in planning, estimating, budgeting, financing, and controlling costs throughout the project lifecycle.
5. **Project Quality Management:** Processes that incorporate the organization's quality policy to ensure the project meets its objectives and stakeholder expectations.
6. **Project Resource Management:** Processes to identify, acquire, and manage the resources (people and physical equipment) needed for the project.
7. **Project Communications Management:** Processes to ensure timely and appropriate planning, collection, creation, and distribution of project information.
8. **Project Risk Management:** Processes concerned with conducting risk management planning, identification, analysis, response planning, and monitoring.
9. **Project Procurement Management:** Processes to purchase or acquire products, services, or results from outside the project team.
10. **Project Stakeholder Management:** Processes to identify and engage stakeholders and manage their expectations throughout the project.
11. **Knowledge Area Composition:** Each knowledge area contains processes from across the five process groups (Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, Closing).
12. **Interconnected Nature:** Knowledge areas are not isolated; changes in one area (e.g., scope) typically impact others (e.g., time, cost, risk).

## 3.5 Define Project Portfolio Management
Project Portfolio Management (PPM) is a strategy that enables organizations to align their projects, programs, resources, and initiatives with corporate business objectives.
1. **Financial Asset Analogy:** It treats IT assets and projects as financial assets, running projects in a project-oriented business manner similar to investment portfolios.
2. **Integrated Management Areas:** PPM enables integrated management of pipeline and scope, time/resources/skills, cost/procurement/communication, and reporting/forecasting/risk.
3. **Risk Reduction:** Just as a financial portfolio diversifies investments to balance risk, a project portfolio balances high-risk/high-reward with low-risk/stable projects.
4. **Return Optimization:** Selects projects that collectively deliver the greatest business value to the organization.
5. **Balance Maintenance:** Ensures a healthy mix of strategic growth projects, maintenance projects, and compliance initiatives.
6. **Resource Allocation Across Projects:** Manages resource allocation across multiple projects rather than focusing on individual project needs.
7. **Strategic Alignment:** Ensures every project in the portfolio directly supports organizational strategic business objectives.

## 3.8 Define project management office.

2. **Tailoring:** Involves adapting standard methodologies (like PMBOK) to fit the specific needs of the organization and project.
3. **Continuous Improvement:** Project management practice evolves over time as lessons learned are incorporated into future projects.

# 4 Project Integration Management
## 4.1 Project Integration management
1. Definition: Project Integration Management includes the processes needed to identify, define, combine, unify, and coordinate the various processes and project management activities within the project management process groups.
2. Central Coordination Role: Project managers must coordinate all the knowledge areas (scope, time, cost, quality, etc) throughout a project's life cycle.
3. Cross-Process Integration: Ensures that all individual processes (initiating, planning, execution, monitoring and control, closing) work together seamlessly.
4. Application Duration: These actions should be applied from the start of the project to completion, not at just specific phases.
---
## 4.2 Project Charter
1. **Definition**: A project charter is a formal, high-level document that officially authorizes the existence of a project, outlining its purpose, goals, scope and key stakeholders
2. **Authorization Function**: The project charter authorizes the project manager to start the project and to use organizational resources as required
3. **Agreement Contract**: Serves as an agreement contract between the project team and sponsor team, documenting the project's terms and expectations.
4. **Business Need**: Addresses the business needs, high-level description, and product requirements that justify undertaking the project
5. **Project Purpose/justification**: explains why the project is being undertaken, including the business case and return on investment
6. **Assigned Project Manager**: Identifies the project manager by name and defines their authority level for decision making and resource allocation
7. **Summary Milestone Schedule**: Provides high-level timeline with key milestone dates for major deliverables and phase completions
8. **Summary Budget**: Includes the high-level budget estimate and funding sources for the project.
9. **Stakeholder Information**: lists key stakeholders, their influence levels, functional organization participation, and mode of interaction.
10. **Assumption and Constraints**: Documents external and environmental assumptions (e.g., resource availability) and constraints (e.g., fixed deadline)
11. **Success Criteria**: Specifies the evaluation criteria for determining project success, including tangible and intangible benefits
12. **Enterprise Environmental Factors**: Considers culture, infrastructure, tools, human resources, personnel policies and marketplace conditions.
13. **Should include**:
	- Requirements that satisfy customer, sponsor, and other stakeholder needs, wants and expectation
	- business needs, high level project description or product requirements that project is undertaken to address
	- project purpose or justification
	- assigned project manager and authority level
	- summary milestone schedule
	- stakeholder influences
	- functional organization and their mode of participation
	- organizational, environment and external assumptions/constraints
	- business case justifying the project, including Return on investment
	- summary budget 
---
## 4.3 Development of Project Charter
### 4.3.1 Inputs
1. **Contract/ Agreement**: includes much of the information needed for creating a good project charter, such as scope, deliverables, and payment terms
2. **Statement of Work** (SOW): a legally binding document that captures and defines all work management aspects, including business need, product description and strategic plan
3. **Enterprise Environmental Factors**: Culture and infrastructure, tools, human resource policies, personnel policies, and market place conditions.
4. **Organizational Process Assets**: Formal and informal plans, policies, procedures, guidelines, information systems, financial systems, and management systems.
5. **Historical Information**: lessons learned from previous projects and historical data that can influence project success.
6. **SWOT Analysis Results**: Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats analysis conducted with managers from outside the IT department.
### 4.3.2 Tools and Techniques
1. **Project Selection Methods**: Financial analysis methods including NPV, ROI, and Payback Analysis
2. **Project Management Methodology**: Structured approach to applying processes and procedures for project initiation.
3. **Project Management Information System** (PMIS): Software tools that support project charter development and documentation.
4. **Expert Judgement**: Input from individuals or groups with specialized knowledge, experience, or training in project initiation.
5. **Weighted scoring model**: total weighted score = sum of (weight of each attribute x numerical value of that attribute) for comparing project options
### 4.3.3 Outputs
1. **Project Charter**: the formal document authorizing the project and project manager
2. **Project Need and Opportunity**: Documented Justification for why the project is being undertaken
3. **Key Goals and Objectives**: Clear, SMART objectives that define project success
4. **Basic Project Scope**: High level scope statement describing what is included and excluded
5. **Tangible and Intangible Benefits**: Identified benefits including financial returns, strategic advantages, and non-monetary value.
---
## 4.4 Statement of Work
1. **Definition**: A statement of work (SOW) is a narrative description of products or services to be supplied under contract.
2. **Legally Binding**: the sow is a legally binding document that captures and defines all the work management aspects of a project.
3. Sections
	1. **Business Need Section**: Describes the market demand, customer request, technological advancement, legal requirement, or social need driving the project.
	2. **Product description section**: Provides known outline, characteristics and specifications of the product or service to be delivered
	3. **Strategic Plan Section**: Explains the scope, vision, and goals of the project in alignment with organizational strategy
	4. **Scope of Work Section**: Defines the work that needs to be done in order to complete the project successfully
	5. **Task List Section**: Breaks tasks down into phases with clear sequencing and dependencies
	6. **Project Schedule Section**: Includes timelines, milestones, and deadlines for deliverables
	7. **Project Deliverable Section**: Lists all tangible outputs expected from the project
	8. **Successful Criteria** and **Sign-off Section**: Defines how success will be measured and who must approve completion.
---
## 4.5 Work Breakdown Structure
1. **Definition**: Work Breakdown Structure is the process of systematically breaking down the major project work into smaller, more manageable components and sub-components in hierarchical order
2. **Work Package**: A work package is a task at the lowest level of the WBS that can be assigned, estimated and tracked.
3. **Hierarchical Nature**: The WBS organizes work from the total project level down to individual tasks and sub-tasks
4. **What Dimension**: Defines WHAT work needs to be accomplished to complete the proejct.
5. **How Long Dimension**: Identifies the time required to complete each work package.
6. **Cost Dimension**: Identifies a time-phased budget to complete each work package
7. **Resource Dimension**: Identifies resources needed to complete each work package (manpower, equipment, materials)
8. **Responsibility Dimension**: Identifies the person responsible for each unit of work (WHO)
9. **Monitoring Points**: Identifies milestones for measuring success and tracking progress
10. **Gantt Chart Representation**: The WBS is commonly visualized using a Gantt Chart showing tasks, duration, and dependencies
---
## 4.6 Project Scope
1. **Definition**: Project scope refers to all the work involved in creating the products of the project and the processes used to create them
2. **Scope vs Product**: Project scope is the work that must be done to deliver a product with specified features; product scope is the features and functions themselves
3. **Completion Measurement**: Project Scope Completion is measured against the project plan; product scope completion is measured against product requirements
4. **Inclusion and Exclusion**: Project scope management ensures the project includes all the work required (and only the required work) to complete it successfully.
5. **Progressive Elaboration**: Project scope becomes progressively more detailed as the project moves from initiation through planning to execution
6. **Example**: E commerce website: project scope includes requirements gathering, design, coding, testing, deployment and training activities.
---
## 4.7 Project Scope vs Product Scope
| Aspect                 | Project Scope                                                                               | Product Scope                                                                 |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Definition**         | The work that must be done to deliver a product, service, or result with specified features | The features and functions that characterize a product, service, or result    |
| **Completion Measure** | Measured against the project plan                                                           | Measured against the product requirements                                     |
| **Focus**              | Work, activities, and processes required to create the product                              | Attributes, characteristics, and capabilities of the final deliverable        |
| **Example**            | "Design, code, test, and deploy the login module"                                           | "Login module must support multi-factor authentication and password recovery" |

1. **Project Justification:** To relieve the Exam Control Office from printing semester-end marksheets and provide students with secure, on-demand access to their results.

## 4.9 Project Scope Management Process
1. **Initiation Process**: Beginning a project or continuing to the next phase by obtaining authorization and defining high-level scope.
2. **Scope Planning**: Developing documents (scope management plan) that provide the basis for future project decisions and scope control.
3. **Scope Definition:** Subdividing the major project deliverables into smaller, more manageable components for detailed planning
4. **Create WBS**: Developing WBS by breaking down work into work packages at appropriate detail levels.
5. **Scope Verification**: Formalizing acceptance of the project scope by obtaining sign-off from stakeholders on completed deliverables
6. **Scope Change Control:** Controlling changes to the project scope through a formal change request and approval process
7. **Requirement Gathering**: Capturing functional requirements (day-to-day user needs) and non-functional requirements (performance, security, usability).
8. **Requirement Documentation**: using SMART rule or MoSCoW method (Must have, Should have, could have, would have) to prioritize requirements.
9. **Scope Statement Development**: Creating a document that confirms common understanding of project scope, including justification, deliverables, and objectives
10. **Scope baseline**: Establishing the approved scope statement, WBS and WBS Dictionary as the baseline for measuring performance
11. **Scope Creep Prevention**: Implementing controls to prevent unauthorized additions to scope without corresponding time, cost, and resource adjustments
12. **Stakeholder Alignment**: Ensuring all stakeholders have the same understanding of what products will be produced and what processes will be used.
## 4.10 Integrated Change Control
### 4.10.1 Overview
- **Definition**: Integrated Change control is the process of reviewing all change requests, approving changes, and managing changes to deliverables, organizational process assets, project documents and project management plan
- **Purpose**: Ensures that only approved changes are implemented and that changes are communicated to all stakeholders
- **Central Role**: Acts as the gatekeeper for all changes across all knowledge areas (scope, time, cost quality, etc).
- **Documentation Requirement**: Every change request must be documented, evaluated and tracked regardless of whether it is approved or rejected.
### 4.10.2 Inputs to Integrated Change Control
- **Project Management Plan:** The baseline document that defines how changes will be managed and controlled
- **Requested Changes**: Formal change requests that may arise from issue resolution, error correction, or stakeholder requests
- **Work Performance Information**: Data about schedule progress, completed deliverables, quality compliance, resource utilization, and lessons learned.
### 4.10.3 Tools and Techniques
- **Expert judgement**: input from subject matter experts to assess the feasibility and impact of proposed changes.
- **Change control Meetings**: Regularly scheduled meetings of the change control board (CCB) to review and decide on change requests
- **Configuration Management**: Identifying and documenting functional/physical characteristics of items and controlling changes to configuration items.
- **Project Management Information System** (PMIS): Software tools for tracking change requests, approvals, and implementation status
### 4.10.4 Outputs
1. **Approved Change Requests**: Changes that have been reviewed and authorized for implementation
2. **Rejected Change requests**: changes that are denied, with documentation of reasons for rejection
3. **updated project management Plan**: Revised baselines and plans reflecting approved changes
4. **Updated Project Scope Statement**: Modified scope documentation when scope changes are approved
5. **Recommended Corrective/Preventive Actions**: Actions to bring future performance in line with the plan or to prevent potential deviations

# 5 Project Quality Management
## 5.1 How to improve quality in ICT project?
1. **Define Quality to Match User Needs:** Clearly articulate what quality means from the end-user perspective before development begins.
2. **Broadcast Simple Quality Metrics:** Share easy-to-understand quality measurements with all team members to maintain focus.
3. **Fine Tune Goals to Include Quality:** Incorporate quality objectives alongside schedule and cost goals in project planning.
4. **Get the Requirements Right:** Invest time in thorough requirements gathering and validation to prevent downstream defects.
5. **Test Smarter to Test Less:** Focus testing efforts on high-risk areas and critical functionality rather than exhaustive testing of everything.
6. **Design Applications to Reduce Bug Risks:** Use architectural patterns and coding standards that inherently minimize potential defects.
7. **Optimize the Use of Testing Tools:** Leverage automation, continuous integration, and static analysis tools for efficient quality verification.
8. **Use Maturity Models:** Apply frameworks such as PMMM (Project Management Maturity Model) or CMM (Capability Maturity Model) for process improvement.
---
## 5.2 Define project quality.
1. **Definition - Quality:** Quality refers to how good something is compared to other similar things; it is a degree of excellence.
2. **Quality Formula:** Quality = Performance or Result ÷ Expectations or Requirements.
3. **Fitness for Use:** Quality means the product or service meets the customer's stated and implied needs.
4. **Conformance to Requirements:** Quality is achieved when deliverables meet all specified requirements and acceptance criteria.
5. **Customer Satisfaction:** Quality ultimately means the end-user is satisfied with the product's performance and reliability.
6. **Degree of Excellence:** Quality represents how well the product performs its intended functions compared to alternatives.
7. **Zero Defects Concept:** High quality means minimal to no defects that impact user experience or system functionality.
8. **Continuous Improvement:** Quality is not a one-time achievement but an ongoing pursuit of better processes and outcomes.
---
## 5.3 Is there always a tradeoff between quality and productivity? Explain with IT related example.
### 5.3.1 Argument Against Tradeoff
1. **Not Always a Tradeoff:** Quality and productivity can be complementary rather than competing when processes are properly designed.
2. **Lean Production View:** Lean production focuses on cutting out waste while ensuring quality; waste brings cost, not quality.
3. **Defect Prevention vs. Rework:** Writing quality code first prevents debugging time later, increasing net productivity.
4. **Automated Testing:** Unit tests and CI/CD pipelines catch defects early, reducing manual testing time while improving quality.
5. **Code Reusability:** Quality modular code can be reused across projects, increasing long-term productivity.
6. **Technical Debt Reduction:** Poor quality creates technical debt that slows future development dramatically.
7. **Kaizen Philosophy:** Continuous small improvements enhance both quality and efficiency over time.
8. **Example - Agile Development:** Agile iterations produce working software frequently; quality practices like test-driven development actually speed up delivery by reducing rework.
### 5.3.2 Where Tradeoff Exists 
1. **Time Constraints Exist:** When deadlines are absolutely fixed, teams may sacrifice quality activities (testing, documentation) to meet schedule.
2. **Resource Limitations:** Limited budget may force reduction in quality assurance staffing or testing tools.
3. **Example - E-commerce Launch:** A retail company must launch before Black Friday; they may accept minor UI bugs to hit the critical date.
4. **Example - MVP Development:** A startup may release a minimum viable product with limited features (scope/quality reduction) to test market quickly.
5. **Over-engineering Risk:** Pursuing perfection beyond customer needs wastes time without proportional value.
6. **Diminishing Returns:** After a certain point, additional quality efforts yield minimal improvement while consuming significant time.
7. **Example - Internal Tool:** A one-time data migration script may not need the same quality standards as customer-facing production software.
8. **Balanced Approach:** The project manager must find the optimal point where quality meets requirements without excessive productivity loss.
---
## 5.4 How quality assurance and quality control is implemented
### 5.4.1 Quality Assurance Implementation
1. **Definition:** Quality Assurance (QA) is the process of auditing quality requirements and results from quality control measurements to ensure appropriate quality standards are used.
2. **Planning Phase:** QA begins during planning when the quality management plan is developed, describing how the project team will implement its quality policy.
3. **Process-Focused:** QA focuses on the processes used to create deliverables, not just the deliverables themselves.
4. **Quality Audits:** Structured reviews of project activities to verify compliance with organizational and project policies, processes, and procedures.
5. **Audit Learning Opportunity:** The planning phase of an audit allows auditors to learn about the company's Quality Management System and take a closer look at system documentation.
6. **Continuous Improvement:** QA identifies lessons learned and best practices that can improve processes on current and future projects.
7. **Training Requirements:** QA includes identifying and addressing training needs for team members on quality processes and tools.
8. **Approach to Quality Audit:** Includes verifying documentation, checking process adherence, identifying gaps, and recommending corrective actions.
### 5.4.2 Quality Control Implementation
1. **Definition:** Quality Control (QC) is the process of monitoring specific project results to determine whether they comply with relevant quality standards.
2. **Deliverable Inspection:** QC examines actual deliverables (code modules, designs, documents) against acceptance criteria.
3. **Identifying Causes:** QC identifies ways to eliminate causes of unsatisfactory performance and defects.
4. **Testing of IT Systems:** Includes Unit Testing (individual components), Integration Testing (grouped components), System Testing (entire system), and User Acceptance Testing (end-user validation).
5. **Test Life Cycle:** Follows Requirement Analysis → Test Planning → Test Case Development → Environment Setup → Test Execution → Test Cycle Closure.
6. **Acceptance Decisions:** QC outputs include acceptance decisions (accept/reject deliverables) and rework requests.
7. **Control Charts:** Graphic displays showing process results over time to determine if a process is in control or out of control.
8. **Pareto Charts:** Bar graphs showing defects in descending order to highlight the most common sources of problems (80/20 rule).

## 5.5 Quality Audit Plan
1. **Definition:** A Quality Audit Plan is a document that outlines the systematic examination of a quality system carried out by internal or external auditors.
2. **Responsibility Assignment:** Specifies who is responsible for managing various areas of quality auditing within the project.
3. **Audit Scope:** Defines which processes, deliverables, or phases will be examined during the quality audit.
4. **Audit Frequency:** Establishes the schedule and timing for quality audits throughout the project lifecycle.
5. **Audit Methodology:** Describes the techniques and tools to be used during the audit (interviews, document reviews, process observation).
6. **Documentation Requirements:** Specifies what records, reports, and artifacts will be reviewed during the audit.
7. **Audit Team Composition:** Identifies the auditors (internal staff or external consultants) and their qualifications.
8. **Reporting Format:** Defines how audit findings will be documented, communicated, and tracked to closure.
9. **Corrective Action Process:** Outlines how non-conformances will be addressed, resolved, and verified.
10. **Follow-up Procedures:** Describes how previous audit findings will be re-examined to ensure effective resolution.

## 5.7 What is quality planning, quality assurance and quality control. Explain different approaches to these projects.
### 5.7.1 Quality Planning
1. **Definition:** Quality planning is the process of identifying which quality standards are relevant to the project and determining how to satisfy them.
2. **Project Quality Requirements:** Defines customer expectations for quality in terms of quality factors, quality criteria, and quality metrics.
3. **Cost of Quality Planning:** Estimates costs for prevention (error-free execution), inspection (evaluation), internal failure (correction before delivery), and external failure (correction after delivery).
4. **Quality Management Plan Output:** Describes management responsibility, document management, requirement scope, design control, testing, quality audits, and training requirements.
### 5.7.2 Quality Assurance
1. **Definition:** Quality assurance is the process of auditing quality requirements and results to ensure appropriate quality standards are used.
2. **Process Focus:** QA evaluates whether processes are being followed correctly, not just inspecting final deliverables.
3. **Audit Approach:** Systematic examination of quality system documentation, process adherence, and continuous improvement mechanisms.
4. **Prevention Orientation:** QA aims to prevent defects by improving processes rather than detecting defects after creation.
### 5.7.3 Quality Control
1. **Definition:** Quality control is the process of monitoring specific project results to determine compliance with quality standards.
2. **Inspection Approach:** QC examines actual deliverables (products, code, documents) against acceptance criteria.
3. **Detection Orientation:** QC identifies defects in completed work so they can be corrected before customer delivery.
4. **Outputs:** QC produces acceptance decisions, rework requests, process adjustments, and validated deliverables.

## 5.8 Discuss the quality control process and its major outputs.
### 5.8.1 Quality Control Process
1. **Monitoring Specific Results:** QC examines actual project deliverables and work results against defined quality standards.
2. **Compliance Determination:** Determines whether deliverables comply with relevant quality standards and specifications.
3. **Cause Identification:** Identifies ways to eliminate causes of unsatisfactory performance and recurring defects.
4. **Inspection and Testing:** Uses techniques like unit testing, integration testing, system testing, and user acceptance testing.

4. **Validated Deliverables:** Deliverables that have been inspected, tested, and confirmed to meet requirements.

6. **Updated Organizational Process Assets:** Updated checklists, templates, standards, and lessons learned from quality control activities.

## 5.9 How are control charts used to monitor the quality of project result
1. **Graphic Display:** Control charts are graphic displays of data that illustrate the results of a process over time.
2. **Prevention Focus:** The main use of control charts is to prevent defects, rather than to detect or reject them after they occur.
3. **In-Control vs. Out-of-Control:** Quality control charts allow you to determine whether a process is in control or out of control.
4. **In-Control Process:** When a process is in control, any variations in results are created by random events; such processes do not need to be adjusted.
5. **Out-of-Control Process:** When a process is out of control, variations are caused by non-random events requiring investigation.
6. **Control Limits:** Upper and lower control limits define the acceptable range of variation for a process.
7. **Specification Limits:** May also be plotted to show customer requirements versus actual process capability.
8. **Trend Detection:** Control charts reveal trends, cycles, or patterns that indicate potential problems before defects occur.
9. **Corrective Action Trigger:** Points outside control limits or non-random patterns trigger investigation and corrective action.
10. **Process Improvement Evidence:** Control charts show whether process changes actually improved quality over time.

## 5.11 TQM.
1. **Definition:** Total Quality Management (TQM) is an approach for project quality management that takes everything related to quality under consideration.
2. **Process Management:** The processes that produce services are thoroughly managed, minimizing process variation and providing predictable quality levels.
3. **Organization-Wide Effort:** All members of an organization participate in improving processes, products, services, and the culture in which they work.
4. **Top Management Commitment:** TQM requires strong, visible leadership from senior management to drive quality initiatives.
5. **Customer Orientation:** TQM focuses on meeting or exceeding customer expectations as the primary measure of quality.
6. **Employee Involvement:** All employees are empowered and expected to contribute to continuous improvement.
7. **Continuous Improvement (Kaizen):** TQM embraces ongoing, incremental improvements rather than occasional major changes.
8. **Training Needs:** TQM recognizes that proper training is essential for employees to contribute effectively to quality.
9. **Corporate Culture:** TQM requires a culture shift where quality becomes everyone's responsibility, not just quality department's.
10. **Tools and Techniques:** TQM utilizes Pareto charts, scatter plots, control charts, fishbone diagrams, histograms, check sheets, and workflow analysis.

## 5.13 Write steps to improve quality IT project. / Explain 80/20 rule of the pareto principle with example. / How pareto charts help to achieve better quality project?
### 5.13.1 80/20 Rule (Pareto Principle)
1. **Definition:** The 80/20 rule states that roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes.
2. **Quality Application:** In IT projects, approximately 80% of defects are caused by 20% of the code modules or processes.
3. **Example - E-commerce Portal:** Based on customer complaint data, 80% of complaints come from 20% of complaint types (e.g., slow website speed and unresponsive customer support).
4. **Focused Improvement:** By addressing the top 20% of defect causes, teams can resolve 80% of quality issues efficiently.
5. **Resource Optimization:** Pareto principle helps teams focus quality efforts where they will have the greatest impact.
### 5.13.2 Pareto Chart
1. **Definition:** A Pareto chart is a chart containing both a bar graph and a line graph, with individual values in descending order by bars and cumulative total represented by a line.
2. **Visual Priority Setting:** Bars show individual defect frequencies in descending order, making the largest problems immediately visible.
3. **Cumulative Line:** The line graph shows cumulative percentage, helping identify the point where 80% of issues are covered.
4. **Decision Tool:** Helps project managers decide where to focus quality improvement efforts for maximum impact.
5. **Example Application:** For e-commerce complaints, Pareto analysis might show that addressing "slow website speed" and "unresponsive customer support" alone covers 60%+ of all complaints.

## 5.15 Write short notes on: Cause and Effect Diagram
1. **Definition:** A Cause-and-Effect Diagram (also called Fishbone Diagram or Ishikawa Diagram) is a tool that helps perform cause-and-effect analysis for a problem you are trying to solve.
2. **Structure - Problem Statement:** The effect (problem) is written in a box on the right-hand side of the diagram.
3. **Structure - Backbone:** A horizontal line (the backbone) runs from left to right toward the problem statement.
4. **Major Cause Categories:** Lines branch off the backbone representing major cause categories (Methods, Machines, Materials, People, Measurement, Environment).
5. **Brainstorming Causes:** The team brainstorms all possible causes and places them in appropriate categories.
6. **Deeper Causes:** Each cause is examined for sub-causes, creating multiple levels of branching.
7. **Root Cause Identification:** The final step is identifying root causes by looking for causes that appear repeatedly or using group consensus.
8. **Problem Resolution:** Once root causes are identified, targeted solutions can be developed and implemented.

## 5.17 Describe how resource available, time available and amount of product to be built can be varied during a development process in order to ensure the resulting software is of high quality.
### 5.17.1 Resource Variation
1. **Add More Developers:** Bringing additional skilled developers can parallelize work, allowing more time for quality activities like code reviews and testing.
2. **Add Quality Specialists:** Dedicated QA engineers, security specialists, or performance testers can focus exclusively on quality assurance.
3. **Invest in Tools:** Purchasing static analysis tools, automated testing frameworks, and continuous integration systems improves quality detection.
4. **Training Investment:** Allocating resources to train existing team on quality practices (TDD, code reviews, security) improves long-term quality.
5. **Peer Review Time:** Allocating resource time specifically for design reviews, code reviews, and documentation reviews catches defects early.
6. **Example Trade-off:** Adding two QA engineers may increase cost but reduces defect leakage to production, improving overall quality.
### 5.17.2 Time Variation
1. **Extend Schedule:** Allowing more time for requirements validation, design reviews, and thorough testing improves quality outcomes.
2. **Add Iteration Time:** Longer sprints or iterations allow more time for refactoring, technical debt reduction, and comprehensive testing.
3. **Buffer for Defect Fixing:** Building time buffers specifically for addressing defects found during testing prevents rushed, low-quality fixes.
4. **Extended User Acceptance Testing:** More time for UAT allows users to thoroughly validate functionality and report issues before release.
5. **Example Trade-off:** Extending a 6-month project to 8 months allows dedicated time for security testing and performance optimization that would otherwise be skipped.
### 5.17.3 Product Amount Variation
1. **Reduce Scope (Minimum Viable Product):** Delivering fewer features but with higher quality ensures critical functions work flawlessly.
2. **Defer Non-Essential Features:** Moving low-priority features to future releases allows focus on quality for core functionality.
3. **Simplify Complex Features:** Breaking complex features into simpler, more testable components reduces defect potential.
4. **Remove High-Risk Features:** Eliminating features with high technical risk or unclear requirements prevents quality issues in unstable areas.
5. **Example Trade-off:** An e-commerce site may launch with only payment processing and product search (reduced scope) but ensure those features have 99.99% reliability and zero security defects.

# 6 Project Communication Management
## 6.1 Project communication management (CM)
1. **Definition:** Project communication management is a collection of processes that help make sure the right messages are sent, received, and understood by the right people.
2. **Knowledge Area:** It is one of the 10 key knowledge areas in the PMBOK (Project Management Body of Knowledge).

4. **Holistic Understanding:** Everyone must understand how communications affect the project as a whole.
---
## 6.2 Importance of CM
1. **Greatest Threat:** The greatest threat to many projects is a failure to communicate properly, not technical challenges.
2. **IT Professional Culture:** The culture of IT professionals often does not naturally make them good communicators, requiring deliberate management.
3. **Career Advancement:** Research shows that IT professionals must be able to effectively communicate to succeed in their positions.
4. **Strong Verbal Skills:** Strong verbal skills are a key factor in career advancement for IT professionals.
5. **Project Manager's Time:** 80-90% of a project manager's time is spent communicating in some form.
6. **Bridging Gaps:** Communication management bridges gaps between technical teams, business stakeholders, and end-users with different vocabularies.
7. **Requirement Clarity:** Prevents misunderstandings about requirements that can lead to costly rework and delays.
8. **Stakeholder Alignment:** Ensures all stakeholders have a shared understanding of project goals, progress, and changes.
9. **Issue Resolution:** Enables quick identification and resolution of problems before they escalate.
10. **Success Achievement:** Project managers must understand the importance of good communications as a means of achieving project success.

## 6.6 Project communication planning.
1. **Definition:** Communication planning determines the information and communication needs of the stakeholders throughout the project.
2. **Stakeholder Analysis:** Identifies who needs what information, when they will need it, and how it will be given to them.
3. **Communication Management Plan:** Every project should include a communications management plan that guides project communications.
4. **Number of Communication Channels:** Total channels = n(n-1)/2, where n is number of stakeholders; complexity increases with more people.
5. **Information Requirements:** Determined from organizational charts, project organization, stakeholder responsibility relations, and disciplines involved.
6. **Internal vs. External Needs:** Differentiates between information needed by internal team members vs. external stakeholders.
7. **Formal vs. Informal:** Balances formal communications (reports, meetings) with informal (conversations, emails).
8. **Technology Selection:** Chooses appropriate communication technologies based on stakeholder locations and needs.
9. **Constraints and Assumptions:** Documents communication constraints (security, language) and assumptions (internet access, language proficiency).
10. **Output:** The communication management plan is the primary output of the communication planning process.

## 6.7 Hazards of communication error 
1. **Requirement Misunderstanding:** Incorrectly interpreted requirements lead to wrong features being built and costly rework.
2. **Missed Deadlines:** Poor communication about task dependencies and progress causes schedule slips.
3. **Budget Overruns:** Undisclosed issues or changes not communicated lead to unplanned expenses.
4. **Stakeholder Discontent:** Stakeholders surprised by negative news or changes lose trust in the project team.
5. **Scope Creep:** Unclear communication about what is included/excluded allows unauthorized additions.
6. **Duplicated Effort:** Team members unaware of each other's work may duplicate tasks or work at cross-purposes.
7. **Risk Realization:** Risks not communicated cannot be mitigated, leading to preventable problems.
8. **Low Team Morale:** Poor communication creates confusion, frustration, and disengagement among team members.
9. **Defect Escalation:** Quality issues not communicated to developers continue to propagate through later stages.
10. **Project Failure:** Severe or cumulative communication errors ultimately lead to complete project failure.

## 6.8 Find the no. of communication channels among 7 people
1. **Formula:** Number of communication channels = n(n-1)/2, where n = number of people.
2. **Calculation:** For n = 7, channels = 7 × (7-1) ÷ 2 = 7 × 6 ÷ 2 = 42 ÷ 2 = 21.
3. **Interpretation:** With 7 people, there are 21 distinct communication channels through which information can flow.
4. **Implication:** As team size grows, communication complexity grows exponentially, requiring deliberate management.

## 6.11 Steps to conduct effective meeting
1. **Determine Necessity:** Before organizing a meeting, think about whether a meeting is really needed or if email/chat would suffice.
2. **Define Purpose and Outcome:** Clearly define the purpose and intended outcome of the meeting before sending invitations.
3. **Determine Attendees:** Identify who needs to attend based on their role and contribution; avoid inviting unnecessary people.
4. **Provide Agenda in Advance:** Send the agenda to participants before the meeting so they can come prepared.
5. **Estimate Time:** Determine how much time is needed and schedule accordingly; stick to the allocated duration.
6. **Prepare Materials:** Prepare handouts, slides, and other materials ahead of time, not during the meeting.
7. **Start on Time:** Start the meeting at the scheduled time regardless of latecomers to respect those who arrived on time.
8. **Run Professionally:** Follow the agenda, manage time, prevent tangents, and ensure all attendees have opportunity to contribute.
9. **Build Relationships:** Use meetings as opportunities to build relationships with attendees, not just exchange information.
10. **Document Outcomes:** Capture decisions, action items, and owners; distribute minutes promptly after the meeting.
---
## 6.12 How to write effective email
1. **Send to Right People:** Be sure to send the email only to people who actually need the information.
2. **Use Meaningful Subjects:** Write clear, specific subject lines that help recipients prioritize and find emails later.
3. **Limit Attachments:** Limit the number and size of attachments; consider links to shared drives for large files.
4. **Keep Messages Concise:** Get to the point quickly; use bullet points for readability.
5. **Clear Call to Action:** State explicitly what you need from recipients (reply by date, approve, review, take action).
6. **Use Professional Tone:** Maintain professional language appropriate for the recipient and organizational culture.
7. **Proofread:** Check for spelling, grammar, and clarity before sending; errors reduce credibility.
8. **Reply Promptly:** Respond to emails quickly, even if only to acknowledge receipt and provide expected response time.
9. **Use Distribution Lists:** Create and use distribution lists for regular communications to avoid missing recipients.
10. **File and Organize:** Respond to and file emails quickly; use folders and filters to manage inbox.
11. **Delete Unnecessary Emails:** Delete email you don't need; don't open suspicious messages from questionable sources.
12. **Maintain Security:** Make sure your virus software is up to date; avoid sending sensitive information unencrypted.

## 6.13 How communication skill help to resolve conflicts
### 6.13.1 How Communication Skills Resolve Conflicts
1. **Active Listening:** Understanding all parties' perspectives before proposing solutions prevents escalation.
2. **Clear Expression:** Articulating issues, impacts, and proposed solutions without ambiguity reduces misunderstanding.
3. **Neutral Language:** Using non-accusatory language focuses discussion on problems, not blame.
4. **Facilitation:** Guiding conflict discussions to ensure all voices are heard and discussion stays productive.
5. **Questioning:** Asking clarifying questions to uncover root causes rather than surface symptoms.

# 7 Risk Management
## 7.1 What are the different sources of risk in IT project?
1. **Market Risk:** Changes in market conditions, customer preferences, or competitor actions that affect project viability.
2. **Financial Risk:** Budget constraints, funding cuts, currency fluctuations, or cost overruns impacting project completion.
3. **Technology Risk:** Rapid technological obsolescence, immature technologies, integration challenges, or platform incompatibilities.
4. **People Risk:** Staff turnover, skill gaps, team conflicts, key person dependency, or insufficient staffing levels.
5. **Structure/Process Risk:** Poor project management processes, unclear roles, inadequate communication, or bureaucratic overhead.
6. **Requirements Risk:** Unclear, incomplete, or constantly changing requirements leading to rework and delays.
7. **Schedule Risk:** Unrealistic timelines, missed milestones, dependency delays, or inaccurate estimation.
8. **Quality Risk:** Defect-prone code, inadequate testing, poor design, or non-compliance with standards.
9. **Security Risk:** Data breaches, unauthorized access, malware attacks, or vulnerabilities in the system.
10. **Legal/Regulatory Risk:** Non-compliance with data protection laws, licensing issues, or contract disputes.
11. **Vendor/Supplier Risk:** Third-party delays, poor quality deliverables, vendor bankruptcy, or integration issues.
12. **Infrastructure Risk:** Unreliable power, network outages, hardware failures, or insufficient hosting capacity.

## 7.9 What are the risk identification techniques?
1. **Brainstorming:** A technique by which a group attempts to generate ideas or find a solution by collecting ideas without thinking and judgment.
2. **Brainstorming Caution:** Don't overuse brainstorming as it can lead to groupthink and fewer ideas over time.
3. **Delphi Technique:** Participants don't have to be in the same place and can participate anonymously; a group of experts write down and share thoughts with a facilitator.
4. **Delphi Process:** Each expert's view is compiled into a summary report by the facilitator, then shared for further refinement in multiple rounds.
5. **Interviewing:** Fact-finding technique for collecting information face-to-face, by phone, email, or instant messaging discussions.
6. **Interview Value:** Interviewing people with similar project experience is an important tool for identifying potential risks.
7. **SWOT Analysis:** Examining the project from Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats perspectives to identify broad risks.
8. **SWOT for Risks:** Helps identify both negative risks (threats) and positive risks (opportunities) that apply to a project.
9. **Nominal Group Technique:** Participants in same room write down risks individually; papers collected and posted on wall.
10. **Nominal Group Prioritization:** Risks are reviewed and prioritized by the group to create a complete list.
11. **Checklist Analysis:** Using historical risk checklists from previous similar projects to identify potential risks.
12. **Assumption Analysis:** Examining project assumptions for validity; invalid assumptions become risks.

## 7.10 Delphi Technique
1. **Definition:** The Delphi technique is a risk identification method with many similarities to brainstorming but with anonymous participation.
2. **Geographical Flexibility:** Participants don't have to be located in the same place and can participate from different locations.
3. **Anonymity:** Participants contribute anonymously, reducing the influence of dominant personalities or seniority bias.
4. **Facilitator Role:** A group of experts write down and share their thoughts about a problem with a facilitator.
5. **Compilation Process:** Each expert's view is compiled into a summary report by the facilitator.
6. **Multiple Rounds:** The summary is shared back with experts for further refinement in subsequent rounds.
7. **Email Facilitation:** Email can be used to facilitate this technique easily across distributed teams.
8. **Complex Decisions:** The technique is used to make decisions about complex issues based on individual opinions.

## 7.12 Quantitative Risk Analysis
1. **Definition:** Quantitative risk analysis is the process of determining numerically the probability of each risk and its consequences on project objectives.
2. **Process Group:** It is part of the planning process group in project management.
3. **Focus:** Identifies which risks require the most attention based on numerical analysis.
4. **Risk Exposure Quantification:** This process quantifies risk exposure and determines the size of cost and schedule contingency reserves that may be needed.
5. **Numerical Output:** Produces numerical probabilities (e.g., 85% chance of completing by December) rather than qualitative rankings (High/Medium/Low).
6. **Tools Used:** Expected Monetary Value (EMV), decision trees, sensitivity analysis, and Monte Carlo simulation.
7. **Prerequisite:** Requires risks to have been already identified and prioritized through qualitative analysis.
8. **Complex Projects:** Used only in complex projects where numerical precision is valuable.
9. **Contingency Calculation:** Helps calculate specific contingency reserves needed for cost and schedule.
10. **Not Always Needed:** Not all projects require quantitative analysis; it is resource-intensive.

## 7.14 Different methods of quantitative analysis of risk.
1. **Sensitivity Analysis (Tornado Analysis):** A technique used to show the effects of changing one or more variables on an outcome; determines which risks have the most impact on the project.
2. **Expected Monetary Value (EMV):** A project management metric used in risk analysis for determining the overall resource plan required; EMV = Probability × Impact.
3. **Decision Tree Analysis:** A diagram that helps identify outcomes due to a collection of related choices; used for EMV study with multiple decision paths.
4. **Monte Carlo Simulation:** A mathematical technique used to estimate the possible outcomes of an uncertain event by running thousands of simulations.
5. **Simulation Steps:** Evaluate range for variables → Determine probability distribution → Select random values → Run deterministic analysis → Repeat many times.
6. **Tornado Diagram:** A special bar chart used in sensitivity analysis where risk data categories are arranged vertically from largest to smallest on horizontal bars.
7. **Modeling and Simulation:** Uses a representation or model of a system to analyze the expected behavior or performance.
8. **Probability Distribution:** Assigning probability distributions (normal, triangular, beta) to uncertain variables for analysis.
9. **Risk Exposure Calculation:** RE = P × C where P is probability and C is cost to the project should the risk occur.
10. **Contingency Reserve Determination:** Quantitative analysis determines specific dollar or time reserves needed for identified risks.
11. **Confidence Levels:** Provides statistical confidence levels for project completion dates and costs (e.g., 85% confidence of completing by December).
12. **Return on Investment Analysis:** Quantifies the financial impact of positive risks (opportunities) on project ROI.

## 7.15 SWOT Analysis
1. **Definition:** SWOT analysis is the process of examining the project from each characteristic's point of view: Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats.
2. **Strengths (Internal Positive):** Attributes of the project or organization that give advantage over others (e.g., experienced team, strong industry contacts, technical skills).
3. **Weaknesses (Internal Negative):** Attributes that place the project at a disadvantage (e.g., no accounting experience, unclear marketing strategy, limited funding).
4. **Opportunities (External Positive):** External factors that could benefit the project (e.g., potential large client, growing market, industry conferences for promotion).
5. **Threats (External Negative):** External factors that could harm the project (e.g., competition, customer preference for established firms, high industry risk).
6. **Risk Identification Use:** SWOT analysis can be used during risk identification to uncover broad negative and positive risks.
7. **Internal vs. External:** Strengths and weaknesses are internal; opportunities and threats are external.
8. **Manager Involvement:** Very important to have managers from outside the IT department assist in the planning process.
9. **Strategic Alignment:** SWOT helps ensure project strategy considers both internal capabilities and external environment.
10. **Example Context:** For a film industry business, strengths include industry contacts and technical skills; threats include competition from established firms.

## 7.16 Sensitivity Analysis.
1. **Definition:** Sensitivity analysis is a technique used to show the effects of changing one or more variables on an outcome.
2. **Risk Impact Identification:** Determines which risks have the most potential impact on project objectives.
3. **Tornado Diagram:** A special bar chart used in sensitivity analysis where risk data categories are arranged vertically from largest to smallest.
4. **Tornado Appearance:** The diagram has a tornado-like form because longer bars at the top and shorter at the bottom create the shape.
5. **Variable Comparison:** Used to compare the relative importance of different variables with high degrees of uncertainty and stability.
6. **One Variable at a Time:** Typically varies one input variable while holding others constant to isolate impact.
7. **Output Sensitivity:** Shows how sensitive the project outcome (cost, schedule) is to changes in each risk variable.
8. **Priority Setting:** Helps project managers focus risk response efforts on variables that cause the most outcome variation.
9. **Decision Support:** Provides quantitative basis for deciding where to allocate contingency reserves.
10. **Assumption Testing:** Tests how changes in assumptions affect project projections.
11. **Risk Ranking:** Ranks risks by their potential impact magnitude, independent of probability.
12. **Complement to Probability:** Used alongside probability analysis; a low-probability but high-sensitivity risk may still need attention.

## 7.18 three typical risks and their countermeasures. 
### 7.18.1 Risk 1: Requirements Changes
- **Description:** Stakeholders request new features or changes to existing functionality after development has begun.
- **Countermeasure 1:** Implement formal change control process requiring impact analysis and approval before any change.

- **Description:** A critical team member with unique technical or domain knowledge leaves the project.

## 7.20 Response strategies for negative risk?
1. **Risk Avoidance:** Eliminating a specific threat, usually by eliminating its causes or changing the project plan to circumvent the risk.
2. **Risk Acceptance:** Accepting the consequences if a risk occurs; can be passive (no action) or active (contingency reserves).
3. **Risk Transference:** Shifting the consequences of a risk and responsibility for its management to a third party (e.g., outsourcing, insurance, warranties).
4. **Risk Mitigation:** Reducing the impact or probability of a risk event to an acceptable threshold.
5. **Avoidance Example:** Choosing a proven technology instead of an experimental one to avoid technical failure risk.
6. **Acceptance Example:** Setting aside contingency budget for potential currency fluctuation losses.
7. **Transference Example:** Purchasing cyber insurance or using a fixed-price contract with a vendor.
8. **Mitigation Example:** Conducting additional testing, implementing redundancy, or cross-training team members.
9. **Escalation:** Notifying a higher-level authority when a risk exceeds the project manager's authority to address.
10. **Contingent Response Strategies:** Developing contingency plans, creating contingency allowance procedures, and planning for alternative development paths.

## 7.21 Tornado Analysis
1. **Definition:** Tornado analysis is a type of simulation modeling used in sensitivity analysis to display the relative importance of different variables.
2. **Visual Format:** The tornado diagram is a special bar chart where risk data categories are arranged vertically from largest to smallest on horizontal bars.
3. **Tornado Appearance:** The diagram has a tornado-like form because longer bars at the top and shorter at the bottom create the shape.
4. **Purpose:** Used to compare the importance (relative) of different variables with a high degree of uncertainty and stability.
5. **Sensitivity Analysis Display:** Displays sensitivity analysis results to compare relative importance and impact of variables.
6. **Risk Ranking:** Shows which risks have the most potential impact on project outcomes, ranked from highest to lowest.
7. **Horizontal Bars:** Each horizontal bar represents one risk variable; bar length indicates the range of possible outcomes.
8. **Vertical Line:** A vertical line represents the base case or expected value for comparison.
9. **Priority Setting:** Helps project managers focus risk response efforts on the top (longest bar) risks.
10. **Decision Support:** Provides visual, intuitive understanding of which variables most affect project success.
11. **Complement to Probability:** Tornado shows impact magnitude; combine with probability for complete risk prioritization.
12. **Common in Monte Carlo Outputs:** Often generated as an output of Monte Carlo simulation tools.

## 7.22 Decision Tree vs Tornado Analysis 
| Aspect                   | Decision Tree Analysis                                              | Tornado Analysis                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Primary Purpose**      | Evaluates sequential decisions with multiple branches and outcomes  | Compares sensitivity of outcomes to changes in individual variables |
| **Visual Format**        | Tree diagram with branches representing decisions and chance events | Bar chart with horizontal bars ranked by length (tornado shape)     |
| **Key Output**           | Expected Monetary Value (EMV) for each decision path                | Ranked list of variables by impact on project outcome               |
| **Probability Handling** | Incorporates probabilities at chance nodes                          | Typically shows range of outcomes without probability weighting     |
| **Best For**             | Decisions with multiple stages and discrete choices                 | Identifying which variables have greatest influence on results      |
| **Complexity**           | Becomes complex with many branches                                  | Simpler, focuses on one variable at a time                          |
